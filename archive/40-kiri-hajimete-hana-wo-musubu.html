Paulownia trees bear first seeds.

Kiri hajimete hana wo musubu

The paulownia trees, having flowered in spring, now form their first seed pods as the summer heat reaches its zenith.

The air sits heavy and still, saturated with moisture that clings to skin and leaf alike. High in the paulownia trees, where fragrant purple blossoms hung just weeks ago, green seed capsules begin to swell in the white heat. This is Taisho at its most intense — cicadas cry without pause, and even shadows offer little respite from the shimmering days.

Nature notes

Higurashi cicadas join the chorus now, their melancholy evening calls layering over the daytime drone of aburazemi. Dragonflies patrol the still edges of ponds, and fireflies have given way to the flash of lightning bugs in mountain villages. The rice paddies have deepened to a vivid green, the stalks robust and beginning to form their first grain heads.

Cultural note

This period often encompasses Doyo no Ushi no Hi, the midsummer Day of the Ox, when eating eel is believed to fortify the body against natsubate — summer fatigue. The custom dates to the Edo period, when a scholar reportedly advised an eel seller to promote his wares on this auspicious day. Shops still display banners and lines form early for prized wild unagi.
